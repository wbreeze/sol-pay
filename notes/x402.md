## They are not the same kind of object

x402 is a wire protocol: an HTTP challenge-response -- `402` carrying a JSON
`accepts` array of `PaymentRequirements`, then the client's retry carrying an
`X-PAYMENT` header -- plus a facilitator API (`/verify`, `/settle`,
`/supported`), plus per-chain *schemes* that say what the payload means. It
specifies no program of its own. On Solana the `exact` scheme asks only that
the client's partially-signed transaction contain exactly one matching
`TransferChecked` to the recipient's associated token account, with a Memo
instruction for replay uniqueness.

sol-pay is the other half of that division. An on-chain program with
persistent state, plus libraries, and deliberately no HTTP surface at all
(SPEC §1: the library does not route, render, format, or decide).

x402 standardizes the conversation and leaves the money movement to a plain
transfer. sol-pay standardizes the money movement and leaves the conversation
to the integrator. Which is why most of the comparisons that suggest
themselves are category errors, and why only one of the differences below is
load-bearing.

## The difference that generates the others

**Where the authorization lives.**

x402 keeps it in a single-use signed message. `exact` is one signed transfer
for one resource. `upto` -- the closest thing in x402 to a spending limit, and
the scheme to read before assuming there is no overlap -- authorizes a maximum
and settles for anything up to it, including zero, and is then spent: "Each
authorization MUST be settled at most once. After settlement (regardless of
amount), the authorization is consumed and cannot be reused." It documents EVM
chains only, as of this reading.

sol-pay keeps it in chain state: the SPL delegation of the meter PDA for
the full limit, and a `Meter` account carrying `limit`, `used` and `paid`
that outlives the request.

Five consequences follow, in the order they matter to this repository.

**1. Who has to be present.** `meter_and_settle` is signed by the site
authority alone, reader absent, and the program enforces that
(`has_one = authority`). Every x402 payment needs a fresh payer signature for
that request. That is unremarkable for a client that is software holding a hot
key, and impossible for a human on their third article. x402's shape assumes
an agent; this program's shape exists to get the reader's wallet out of the
per-view path once the meter is set. This is the whole of the difference in
audience -- agents and APIs against returning human readers -- and every other
line in this note is downstream of it.

**2. Pre-paid turnstile against post-paid meter.** x402 serves nothing until
settlement confirms, so the resource server carries no credit risk. sol-pay
accrues and collects on the threshold, floats the unpaid residue, and forgives
it on close. That is a cost x402 does not have, and SPEC §4.6 already records
it as a trade the site makes against its own float.

**3. Per-request on-chain writes.** x402 settles on chain per paid request.
The tempting claim here is the wrong one, and §4.6 already says why: the
collection threshold saves neither transactions nor fees, because the transfer
is a CPI inside the instruction that raises `used`. What it buys is that nine
calls in ten stay off the treasury account and out of the runtime's write
queue for it, that nine in ten cannot be refused, and that a Token-2022
transfer fee or hook is paid per settle rather than per view. x402's
per-request settlement forgoes all three by construction: every payment writes
the merchant's token account, every request can be refused mid-read, and every
request pays whatever the mint charges per transfer. Note that the first of
those is the contention argument, which §4.6 labels reasoning rather than
measurement; it is no better measured here. On Solana the fee argument
evaporates for both designs. On Base, where the FAQ puts an on-chain fee near
a cent, it does not: a cent against the dime that
`content/MeteredPayEconomics.md` argues for is ten percent of the charge.

**4. Third parties.** x402's facilitator is permissionless to self-host, and
the FAQ says so, but the ordinary integration delegates `/verify` and
`/settle` to a hosted one. The SVM scheme adds a second party with a real
obligation: a sponsor co-signs as `feePayer` and funds the SOL, and the scheme
constrains that account so it authorizes fees and nothing else. sol-pay has no
third party in the path -- the site's own server signs and submits, and the
sidecar §3.1 decided has not been built.

**5. Identity.** x402 needs no session; the payload is the identity, for that
request. sol-pay requires the viewer-to-wallet mapping, and sometimes the
token account too, which §4.2 gave up statelessness to get. For an agent
x402's property is plainly better. For a site that already logs people in,
§4.1's one sentence costs nothing.

Neither design hides reading activity from someone watching the chain. A
counting call is a public transaction naming the meter PDA, so the timeline
is on chain either way, and `used` being an aggregate does not change that.
§4.3 says the right thing already and should keep saying only that: what is
public is public, and the privacy claim worth making is about what the *site*
stores.

## What the facilitator sees

x402's facilitator is not merely a party in the path. It is a party the
protocol hands a reading log to. `POST /verify` and `POST /settle` carry the
same body, and it contains both halves of the join this project refuses:

- `payload.authorization.from` -- the payer's wallet address
- `resource.url`, and with it `resource.description` and `resource.mimeType`
- `amount`, `asset` and `payTo` -- what was charged, and to whom
- a timestamp, implicitly, because it is an HTTP request

So for each paid request a facilitator learns that this wallet paid this much
for this URL on this site at this moment. Twice over, if the server calls
verify and then settle.

Set that against what the chain discloses. An observer of the ledger sees a
wallet, a treasury, an amount and a time. **The URL is not there.** A
facilitator therefore holds strictly more than the public ledger does: it
holds the title. That is the field the demonstrator's privacy page works
hardest to keep out of a profile, and it is the field x402 puts in the
payload.

And it is cross-site. A wallet paying three sites that share one facilitator
has its reading assembled in one place, by a party none of those sites had to
tell anything. That is the join `content/privacy.md` names and refuses --
"readers of this also read that" -- except that here the site cannot refuse it
on the reader's behalf, because the site is not the one performing it, and
could not perform it itself.

The specification says nothing about any of this. No retention rule, no
logging rule, no confidentiality obligation, no statement of what a
facilitator may do with what it receives, and no threat model in which the
facilitator is an adversary. It defines the endpoints and stops.

Which is the only honest answer to whether a facilitator mines that data: the
protocol does not say, because it constrains nothing either way. It is
whatever each operator's terms say, resting on a flow that delivers a
per-wallet reading log across all of that operator's customers. Any particular
operator's incentives are probably elsewhere, and their terms probably say
something reassuring. That is a guess about a company rather than a property
of the standard, and `content/privacy.md`'s whole method is to decline that
guess on the reader's behalf.

Unverified as of this writing: hosted facilitators appear to gate access
behind API keys, which would also tell the facilitator which merchant each
request came from. Not checked. It changes nothing either way, since `payTo`
names the merchant already.

The escape exists, and the FAQ names it -- facilitators are permissionless and
anyone may run one. But self-hosting gives back exactly what x402 was
offering, the chain work behind someone else's HTTP boundary, and leaves a
service to operate. Which is §3.1's sidecar, and the next section.

## Where this bears on §3.1

This is the part with consequences for the work rather than for the README.

x402 dissolves §3.1's problem instead of solving it. Any language that can set
an HTTP header can be a resource server, because the chain work sits behind
the facilitator's HTTP boundary. No port, no second implementation of the
encoding, none of §8.1's machinery to keep one honest.

And §3.1's own decided answer -- a sidecar with a documented HTTP interface,
"a signing oracle, so it is simultaneously the reach answer and the custody
answer" -- is a facilitator under another name. Not the same protocol, and it
serves the site authority rather than a payer, but the same object in the
architecture. The trust boundary §3.1 says is undesigned is the boundary x402
has already had to draw, twice, for two chains. When that gets designed, the
facilitator interface is prior art to read, and `/verify` + `/settle` is a
shape to deviate from knowingly rather than to reinvent by accident.

**One design constraint follows from the section above.** The sidecar must
take an instruction to sign, never a request to authorize. An interface that
accepts anything shaped like *here is the URL this reader wants, tell me
whether they may have it* would give a sidecar run by anyone but the site
x402's disclosure with none of its standardization. Keeping the resource's
identity on the site's side of that socket is what keeps the boundary about
custody, which is what §3.1 says it is for.

Worth recording beside it: `meter_and_settle` carries no URL, so a title never
reaches the chain -- and the demonstrator still had to coarsen its per-article
counters, because a live count beside a ledger of timestamped payments can
match a payment to a title on a quiet article (`content/privacy.md`). This
design works to keep the title out of an *aggregate*. x402 puts it in the
payload.

## Does a site that runs one have reason to run the other?

Mostly no: they answer different questions, and neither is a substitute for
the other's half.

There is one honest intersection. x402's scheme registry is extensible, and
"open, renew, or draw on a sol-pay meter" is expressible as a scheme. What
that would buy is the one vocabulary this library has no way to speak: a
standard advertisement of price and terms to a client that has never seen the
site before. It would not change who signs, and it would not remove the
viewer-to-wallet mapping. It is worth something only if agents are ever
readers here, and the market evidence for agent demand is thin -- CoinDesk,
2026-03-11, reported that x402's demand had not materialized. Parking it as a
possibility rather than a plan.

## What to do with this

Nothing in the README yet. A reader of the README is deciding whether to
integrate a metering library, and none of the above changes that decision: the
choice between a standing delegated meter and a per-request turnstile is made
by whether the payer is a person or a program, which an integrator knows
before arriving. If x402 gains a scheme that reaches this program, or if the
sidecar's interface ends up shaped by the facilitator API, that is the point
at which the README owes a paragraph, and this note is what it would be
written from.
